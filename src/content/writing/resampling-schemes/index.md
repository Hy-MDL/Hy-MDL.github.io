## Abstract

Resampling is the step that keeps a particle filter from degenerating, and by 2005 four schemes were in common use with a widespread folk belief that systematic resampling — one uniform draw, $n$ evenly spaced strata — was the best of them. This note settles what can be settled. It fixes the class under study by three constraints: the resampled population has fixed size $n$, all resampled weights are $1/n$, and the duplication counts are unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[N^i\mid\mathcal{G}^n]=n\omega^i$. Within that class it proves, by one application of Jensen's inequality each, that **residual** and **stratified** resampling have conditional variance no larger than multinomial, and that the two compose. Then it exhibits a two-point configuration on which systematic resampling has conditional variance $(\omega-\tfrac12)(1-\omega)\lvert f\rvert^2$ — *larger* than multinomial for most $\omega$, and, more damningly, **not decreasing in $n$ at all**. Finally it establishes a central limit theorem for the bootstrap filter under residual resampling, with an asymptotic variance that decomposes into a resampling term and an importance-sampling term, under a technical condition the authors themselves call non-trivial.

**Keywords:** multinomial resampling, residual resampling, stratified resampling, systematic resampling, conditional variance, central limit theorem, particle filter

## 1 Introduction

Sequential Monte Carlo is *the combination of two main elements: sequential importance sampling* — which the paper traces to Hammersley and Handscomb and to Handschin and Mayne in the 1960s — *and resampling, whose importance in the context of SMC was first demonstrated by* [the bootstrap filter](/blog/bootstrap-filter/). The first element alone gives SIS, whose defect is that it *becomes unstable as $k$ increases due to the discrepancy between the weights*: weight degeneracy. Resampling is the fix, and this paper is about how to do it.

The setup is sequential importance sampling with resampling (SISR). At time 0, draw $m$ particles $\xi_0^i$ from a density $r_0$ with weights $\omega_0^i=\nu_0(\xi_0^i)g_0(\xi_0^i)/r_0(\xi_0^i)$. Thereafter simulate $\xi_{k+1}^i\sim r(\xi_k^i,\cdot)$ and update

$$
\omega_{k+1}^i=\omega_k^i\,\frac{q(\xi_k^i,\xi_{k+1}^i)\,g_{k+1}(\xi_{k+1}^i)}{r(\xi_k^i,\xi_{k+1}^i)}, \tag{1}
$$

with $\nu_0$ the initial state distribution, $q$ the (possibly nonlinear) transition density and $g_k$ the conditional likelihood of the observation given the state. The self-normalised estimator $\sum_i\omega_k^if(\xi_k^i)/\sum_j\omega_k^j$ estimates the filtered moment. The choice $r=q$ collapses (1) to $\omega_{k+1}^i=\omega_k^ig_{k+1}(\xi_{k+1}^i)$ — the bootstrap filter, where the weight is just the likelihood.

The reason a seven-page theory note earns a place in this series is that resampling is the one design choice in a particle filter that is *free*: every scheme costs $O(n)$, every scheme is a few lines of code, and the difference between the best and worst is a variance factor that you pay on every time step forever. Choosing multinomial because it is what the 1993 paper wrote down is a pure loss. For filtering a stochastic-volatility or nonlinear term-structure model over thousands of daily observations, that compounding matters more than most modelling choices.

## 2 Background: the class of schemes being compared

Write $\{\xi^i,\omega^i\}_{1\le i\le m}$ for the weighted particle system at a generic time, normalised so $\sum_i\omega^i=1$, and $\mathcal{G}^n$ for the $\sigma$-field generated by all particles and weights up to and including that time. Resampling produces $\{\tilde\xi^i,\tilde\omega^i\}_{1\le i\le\tilde M}$ with reduced weight discrepancy. The paper restricts to random resampling satisfying

$$
\tilde M=n,\qquad \tilde\omega^i=1/n,\qquad \mathbb{E}\bigl[N^i\mid\mathcal{G}^n\bigr]=n\omega^i, \tag{2}
$$

where $N^i=\#\{j\le n:\tilde\xi^j=\xi^i\}$ are the duplication counts. The third condition is *sometimes known as the "unbiasedness" or "proper weighting" condition*. Note that $n$ and $m$ are kept separate — the population size need not be preserved, and §4 shows that the ratio matters.

Two families are explicitly excluded: schemes where the population size is random after resampling, and schemes where the weights are not equal afterwards. That second exclusion rules out most of what is now called *partial* or *optimal* resampling, so the comparison here is of the classical four.

## 3 The four schemes

> **Key idea.** Every scheme is inverse-CDF sampling from the weight distribution. They differ only in how the $n$ uniforms are generated: independently (multinomial), one per stratum (stratified), one per stratum but all offset by a common shift (systematic), or not at all for the integer part of $n\omega^i$ (residual). The more structure you put into the uniforms, the lower the variance — and the harder the analysis.

### 3.1 Multinomial

Draw $n$ independent $U^i\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1]$, set $I^i=D_\omega^{\mathrm{inv}}(U^i)$ where $D_\omega^{\mathrm{inv}}(u)=i$ for $u\in\bigl(\sum_{j<i}\omega^j,\sum_{j\le i}\omega^j\bigr]$, and take $\tilde\xi^i=\xi^{I^i}$. The counts are $\mathrm{Mult}(n;\omega^1,\dots,\omega^m)$. This is the scheme of the bootstrap filter.

### 3.2 Residual

Take the integer part deterministically and resample only the remainder:

$$
N^i=\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor+\bar N^i,
\qquad
\bar N\sim\mathrm{Mult}\bigl(n-R;\bar\omega^1,\dots,\bar\omega^m\bigr),
\qquad
R=\sum_{i}\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor,
\quad
\bar\omega^i=\frac{n\omega^i-\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor}{n-R}. \tag{3}
$$

A particle with weight $\omega^i\ge 1/n$ is guaranteed at least $\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor$ copies. Unbiasedness is immediate because the fractional parts are resampled multinomially with the right probabilities.

### 3.3 Stratified

Partition $(0,1]=(0,1/n]\cup\cdots\cup((n-1)/n,1]$ and draw $U^i\sim\mathcal{U}\bigl(((i-1)/n,\,i/n]\bigr)$ independently, then invert as before. The structure buys the guarantee that $\lvert N^i-n\omega^i\rvert<1$ for every $i$ — the same near-deterministic property residual has. Unbiasedness follows because

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\tilde\xi^i)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}^n\right]
=n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}f\circ\xi\circ D_\omega^{\mathrm{inv}}(u)\,du
=n\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega^if(\xi^i),
$$

the strata simply reassembling the whole unit interval.

### 3.4 Systematic

Take stratified and make the $n$ draws perfectly dependent:

$$
U^i=\frac{i-1}{n}+U,\qquad U\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1/n]. \tag{4}
$$

One random number for the whole resampling step. The marginals are unchanged, so (2) still holds. The paper notes its history — introduced to the particle-filter literature by Carpenter, Clifford and Fearnhead *as "stratified" sampling*, and known earlier as *universal sampling* — and its appeal: *it is often preferred due to its computational simplicity and good empirical performance.*

And then the sentence that governs everything after: *it is the only resampling method for which the resulting particle positions $\tilde\xi^i$ are no more independent given $\mathcal{G}^n$. Thus, studying its performance is much harder than for other methods.*

### 3.5 Order dependence

*Both stratified and systematic resampling are sensitive to the order in which the particles are ordered: a simple permutation of the indices of the particles before resampling changes the distribution of the new resampled set of particles. In contrast, residual resampling behaves more like the basic multinomial resampling approach in that it disregards the order in which the particles are numbered.*

This is easy to miss and it is the hinge of §4.2. Stratified and systematic read the weights as a sequence, not a set.

```text
n = 8 particles, weights shown as widths on (0,1]
|--- w1 ---|- w2 -|------ w3 ------|- w4 -|-- w5 --|- w6 -|-- w7 --|- w8 -|

MULTINOMIAL   x   x      x   x  x        x      x        x      (8 iid uniforms)
STRATIFIED    | x  | x  |  x |  x |  x  | x  |  x  |  x  |      (1 uniform per stratum)
SYSTEMATIC    | x  | x  | x  | x  | x  | x  | x  | x  |         (1 uniform, then +1/n each)
RESIDUAL      floor(n*w_i) copies guaranteed, remainder drawn multinomially
```

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Conditional variance: residual and stratified win

**Multinomial.** The indices are conditionally i.i.d., so

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\tilde\xi^i)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}^n\right]
=\frac1n\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega^if^2(\xi^i)-\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega^if(\xi^i)\right]^2\right\}. \tag{5}
$$

**Residual.** The estimator splits into a $\mathcal{G}^n$-deterministic piece and a conditionally i.i.d. piece,

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\tilde\xi^i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor}{n}f(\xi^i)+\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n-R}f(\xi^{\bar I^i}), \tag{6}
$$

so only the second contributes variance. The comparison with (5) is the pretty step. Write $\sum_i\omega^if(\xi^i)=\sum_i\frac{\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor}{n}f(\xi^i)+\frac{n-R}{n}\sum_i\bar\omega^if(\xi^i)$, and observe that **the $m$ numbers $\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor/n$ together with $(n-R)/n$ sum to one** — they are themselves a probability distribution on $m+1$ atoms. Jensen's inequality applied to the square then gives

$$
\left\{\sum_i\omega^if(\xi^i)\right\}^2\le\sum_i\frac{\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor}{n}f^2(\xi^i)+\frac{n-R}{n}\left\{\sum_i\bar\omega^if(\xi^i)\right\}^2,
$$

which is exactly what is needed: *the conditional variance of residual sampling is always smaller than that of multinomial sampling.*

**Stratified.** Same conclusion, one line of Jensen applied to the stratum integrals:

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[n\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}f\circ\xi\circ D_\omega^{\mathrm{inv}}(u)\,du\right]^2
\ \ge\
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}f\circ\xi\circ D_\omega^{\mathrm{inv}}(u)\,du\right]^2
=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega^if(\xi^i)\right]^2,
$$

so the subtracted term is larger and the variance is smaller.

**Two corollaries the paper states and that are easy to overlook.** First, the schemes *compose*: applying stratified sampling to the $n-R$ residual draws *can then only decrease the conditional variance* further, so residual-stratified dominates both. Second, *the fact that the conditional variance is reduced does not depend on the particular choice of the sub-intervals* — equal strata are convenient, not necessary, and a weight-adapted partition is legal.

### 4.2 Systematic: the counter-example

Take $n$ even and a particle population consisting of two distinct values *interleaved*:

$$
\{\xi^i\}_{1\le i\le n}=\{x_0,x_1,x_0,x_1,\dots,x_0,x_1\},
$$

with each of the $n/2$ copies of $x_1$ carrying weight $2\omega/n$ and each copy of $x_0$ carrying $2(1-\omega)/n$, for $\tfrac12\le\omega<1$. Write $\lvert f\rvert=\lvert f(x_1)-f(x_0)\rvert$.

**Multinomial**, from (5):

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac1n\sum_if(\tilde\xi^i_{\mathrm{mult}})\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}^n\right]=\frac1n(1-\omega)\,\omega\,\lvert f\rvert^2. \tag{7}
$$

**Residual and stratified** coincide here (they do not in general). Since $2\omega/n>1/n$, both deterministically place $n/2$ particles at $x_1$ and draw the other $n/2$ by conditionally independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $2\omega-1$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}=\frac1n(2\omega-1)(1-\omega)\lvert f\rvert^2, \tag{8}
$$

always below (7), and — the nice observation — *for specific configurations of the weights, such as when $\omega$ gets close to $0.5$, the resampling becomes quasi-deterministic when using residual or stratified resampling and the improvement over the basic multinomial scheme becomes all the more significant.* At $\omega=1/2$ the variance is exactly zero while multinomial's is $1/(4n)\lvert f\rvert^2$.

**Systematic.** It too places $n/2$ particles at $x_1$ deterministically. But because a single shift $U$ drives all $n$ strata and the particles alternate, the remaining $n/2$ slots are *all* resolved the same way: all $x_1$ with probability $2\omega-1$, all $x_0$ with probability $2(1-\omega)$. One Bernoulli draw, scaled by $n/2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac1n\sum_if(\tilde\xi^i_{\mathrm{syst}})\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}^n\right]=\Bigl(\omega-\tfrac12\Bigr)(1-\omega)\lvert f\rvert^2. \tag{9}
$$

**There is no $1/n$.** The conditional variance *does not even decrease to zero as $n$ grows*, and it exceeds (7) for most $\omega$ — the exception being $\omega$ very close to $1/2$, where (9) vanishes faster than (7) does.

The authors are careful about what this does and does not show. The construction depends on the interleaved ordering; *it is easy to verify (using simulations) that, in this example, systematic resampling becomes very similar to residual/stratified resampling if the particles are randomly permuted before resampling*, and *the above counter-example probably corresponds to a "rare" situation*. The conclusion they draw is the right-sized one: systematic resampling *is a variance reduction method which is not as robust as* stratified and residual, and *theoretical study of the behavior of systematic resampling probably is a very hard task*.

The practical reading is a one-line safeguard: if you use systematic resampling, permute the particles first. It costs nothing and removes the only known failure mode.

### 4.3 A central limit theorem

§4 restricts to the bootstrap filter ($r=q$) with resampling at every step, and decomposes an iteration into *prediction* ($\xi_{k+1}^i\sim q(\tilde\xi_k^i,\cdot)$ conditionally independently) and *filtering* (weights $\omega_{k+1}^i=g_{k+1}(\xi_{k+1}^i)/\sum_jg_{k+1}(\xi_{k+1}^j)$, then resample). Resampling affects only the second.

**Assumption 1.** (i) The current particles are consistent for a density $\nu$ and satisfy a CLT with asymptotic variance $\sigma^2(f)$ for all bounded $f$. (ii) The weights come from $g=\mu/\nu$ for a probability density $\mu$, with $g$ bounded above and *known up to a constant only*.

**Theorem 2 (prediction).** New particles drawn conditionally independently from $q$ are consistent for $\nu q$ with

$$
\sigma_+^2(f)=\nu\bigl[qf^2-(qf)^2\bigr]+\sigma^2(qf). \tag{10}
$$

The paper reads this as *a simple instance of the Rao–Blackwell theorem*: the first term is the variance introduced by sampling the transition, the second is what was already there, propagated through the conditional expectation $qf$.

**Theorem 3 (resampling).** If (a) the resampled particles are conditionally independent given $\mathcal{G}^n$, (b) $n\to\infty$ with $n/m\to\alpha$, and (c) $n\operatorname{Var}[\frac1n\sum_if(\tilde\xi^i)\mid\mathcal{G}^n]\to_P\kappa(f)$ deterministic, then a CLT holds for $\mu$ with

$$
\tilde\sigma^2(f)=\kappa(f)+\alpha\,\sigma^2\!\left(\frac{\mu}{\nu}\bigl[f-\mu(f)\bigr]\right). \tag{11}
$$

Two things to take from (11). *The limit of the conditional variance of resampling gets added to the variance of (self-normalized or Bayesian) importance sampling scaled by the factor $\alpha$* — so the two error sources are additive and separately attackable, which is exactly why improving the resampling scheme is worth doing independently of improving the proposal. And the $\alpha$ factor means *using $n\ll m$ may render the variance of the particle estimator almost independent of what happened in previous steps* — immediately followed by the correct caution: *this phenomenon should not be over-interpreted however as it only occurs because the sum is normalized by $n$, and not $m$ (or $m+n$) which is more connected with the actual number of operations required.* Shrinking the population makes the *normalised* variance look good and does not make the estimator better per unit of work.

**Condition (a) is where systematic resampling falls out of the theory entirely.** Its resampled positions are conditionally dependent, so Theorem 3 does not apply, and no CLT is available.

**Instantiating $\kappa$.** For multinomial, (5) plus consistency gives immediately $\kappa(f)=\mu(f^2)-[\mu(f)]^2$ — the variance under the target. *For other resampling schemes however, showing that (11) holds is all but trivial.* The paper does residual, and the obstruction is the floor function: sums like $\sum_i\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor f(\xi^i)/n$ are not continuous functionals of the weights. Lemma 4 handles them under an extra condition,

$$
\mu\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\bigl\{x:\ \alpha\tfrac{\mu}{\nu}(x)\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}\Bigr)=0, \tag{12}
$$

— the set where $\alpha$ times the weight function lands exactly on an integer must have target probability zero — giving $\sum_i\frac{\lfloor n\omega^i\rfloor}{n}f(\xi^i)\to_P\nu\bigl(\tfrac1\alpha\lfloor\alpha\tfrac\mu\nu\rfloor f\bigr)$, and Corollary 5 then gives $\kappa(f)$ for residual resampling in closed form. The paper calls (12) *a non trivial condition*, which is fair: it holds for any $\alpha$ off a Lebesgue-null set and for any $\mu/\nu$ with a continuous distribution, but it is a genuine hypothesis rather than a formality, and it exists only because of the floors.

## 5 What is and is not established

Because this is a theory note, the "experiments" section is a list of what the paper proves, what it asserts, and what it leaves alone.

| Scheme | Conditional variance vs multinomial | Conditionally independent given $\mathcal{G}^n$ | Order-sensitive | CLT established here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multinomial | — (baseline) | yes | no | yes, $\kappa(f)=\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)$ |
| Residual | **provably $\le$** | yes | no | yes, under condition (12) |
| Stratified | **provably $\le$** | yes | **yes** | no — (11) *"all but trivial"* to verify |
| Residual + stratified | **provably $\le$ both** | yes | yes | no |
| Systematic | **no** — counter-example (9) | **no** | **yes** | no — Theorem 3 does not apply |

**Asserted without demonstration in this paper.** That systematic resampling has *good empirical performance* in general, and that permuting particles before systematic resampling recovers stratified-like behaviour in the counter-example (*easy to verify (using simulations)* — the simulations are not shown). Both are almost certainly true and neither is shown here.

**Not addressed.**

- **No simulation study of any kind.** There is not a single number from a run of a filter in the paper. Everything is analytic. That is a legitimate choice for a seven-page note, and it means the practically important question — how much does the variance reduction actually buy in filtering RMSE, on a real model, over many steps — is untouched.
- **Computational cost is mentioned only in passing** (systematic is praised for *computational simplicity*) and never quantified. In practice stratified and systematic are single-pass $O(n)$; multinomial is $O(n)$ only if the uniforms are generated in sorted order; residual costs $O(n)$ plus a multinomial on the remainder. The cost differences are small and the paper's silence on them is a small gap given that they are the usual reason people choose systematic.
- **Adaptive resampling is out of scope.** §4 assumes resampling *at each time index*. The effective-sample-size trigger that most implementations use changes the whole analysis, since it makes the number of resampling events random.
- **Only the bootstrap proposal** is treated in the CLT section, *for reasons of space and simplicity*.
- **Excluded families** (§2): random population size, and unequal post-resampling weights. So the increasingly popular partial/optimal resampling schemes are not in the comparison.
- **Path degeneracy is not discussed.** Lower resampling variance at each step does not by itself say anything about the coalescence of ancestral lineages, which is what kills smoothing and parameter estimation.

## 6 Limitations

**Stated by the authors.** That the systematic counter-example depends on particle ordering and *probably corresponds to a "rare" situation*. That the theoretical study of systematic resampling *probably is a very hard task*. That condition (12) is non-trivial. That establishing the variance limit (11) for schemes other than multinomial and residual is *all but trivial*. That the $\alpha$ scaling *should not be over-interpreted*. That $g$ bounded above is assumed but *may be relaxed*.

**My reading.**

- **The most useful result is the negative one**, and it is the one most likely to be misremembered. The finding is not "systematic resampling is bad" — it is "systematic resampling has no dominance guarantee, is order-dependent, and admits configurations where its conditional variance does not vanish with $n$". The remedy is a permutation.
- **Stratified is the practical recommendation and the paper does not quite say so.** It has the variance guarantee, it keeps conditional independence (so the CLT machinery applies in principle), it is single-pass, and it composes with residual. The only cost is order-sensitivity, cured by the same permutation. That this recommendation has to be assembled by the reader from three separate results is a presentational weakness.
- **The interleaved counter-example is less contrived than it looks.** Particle populations are frequently *not* in random order: after a previous systematic resampling, duplicated particles occupy contiguous blocks; after a Rao–Blackwellised or mixture filter, particles may be grouped by discrete regime. Alternating or blocked structure is exactly what arises when a filter tracks two modes. The paper's "rare" is probably right for a generic step and probably wrong for the steps that matter.
- **Condition (12) is an artefact and reads like one.** It exists because $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ is discontinuous at integers; it has no probabilistic meaning. A reader could reasonably conclude that residual resampling's CLT is harder to state than it should be, rather than that residual resampling is delicate.
- **The additive decomposition (11) is the result with the most leverage and gets the least emphasis.** It says resampling error and importance-sampling error do not interact to first order, which is the licence for treating "choose a scheme" and "choose a proposal" as separate problems — the premise of the [auxiliary particle filter](/blog/auxiliary-particle-filter/) and of everything built on it.

## 7 Extensions

**What was built on this.** The variance ordering here became standard practice: stratified and systematic are the defaults in every modern SMC library, and multinomial survives mainly in textbooks and in the [1993 original](/blog/bootstrap-filter/). The analysis of systematic resampling remained open for years and was eventually taken up by Gerber, Chopin and Whiteley, who showed that systematic resampling can indeed fail to be consistent in general and characterised when it is — vindicating the "very hard task" remark. The CLT framework of Theorems 2–3 is the Cappé–Moulines–Rydén machinery, which underpins the asymptotic theory for [SMC samplers](/blog/smc-samplers/) and for the likelihood estimates that make [particle MCMC](/blog/particle-mcmc/) exact. Adaptive resampling on an ESS threshold, which this paper does not treat, became the default and has its own later theory.

**Open problems the paper leaves.** A CLT for stratified resampling, which satisfies the independence hypothesis but whose variance limit is hard to identify. Anything at all for systematic. The right resampling scheme when the weights have heavy tails, where $g$ bounded fails. And the question the paper's scope excludes: how the choice interacts with an ESS-triggered schedule, where resampling happens rarely and each event matters more.

**Research directions.** *These are ideas, not results — none has been run.*

1. **Measure the filtering cost of the variance ordering.** Hypothesis: on a long-horizon nonlinear filtering problem the per-step conditional-variance ordering (multinomial > systematic-permuted ≈ stratified ≈ residual) translates into a filtering-RMSE ordering with the same sign but a much smaller gap, because the resampling variance is dominated by the proposal mismatch term in (11) whenever the observation is informative. Data: the Kitagawa model of the [bootstrap-filter](/blog/bootstrap-filter/) review and a discretised stochastic-volatility model, both over 5,000 steps, 200 replicates. Baseline: all five schemes at matched $n$. Metric: RMSE of the filtered mean and variance of the log-likelihood estimate, plus the empirically measured split of (11) into its two terms. Likely failure mode: the two terms cannot be separated empirically without a reference filter, and at the particle counts where a reference is affordable all five schemes already agree.
2. **Test whether the interleaved counter-example arises in practice.** Hypothesis: after a resampling step, particle populations carry systematic order structure (contiguous duplicate blocks, regime-grouped particles) often enough that unpermuted systematic resampling shows measurably higher variance than stratified on multimodal filtering problems, and the effect grows with the number of modes. Data: a two-regime switching state-space model where the regimes are far apart, at several separations. Baseline: systematic with and without a pre-permutation, versus stratified. Metric: per-step conditional variance of a test function estimated across replicates, and a measure of the post-resampling ordering (runs test on the particle sequence). Likely failure mode: the mutation step between resampling events scrambles the order enough that the structure never survives, making the permutation genuinely unnecessary — which is worth knowing for certain rather than assuming.
3. **Weight-adapted strata.** Hypothesis: the paper's remark that the sub-intervals need not be equal is exploitable — choosing strata boundaries to align with the largest weights (so that a dominant particle is never split across two strata) reduces conditional variance further at negligible cost, most visibly when a few weights dominate, which is the degenerate regime that matters. Data: synthetic weight vectors with controlled Gini coefficient, then a filter run where weight concentration is measured online. Baseline: equal strata (standard stratified) and residual-stratified. Metric: conditional variance of a test function as a function of weight concentration. Likely failure mode: residual resampling already handles exactly the dominant-weight case by taking floors deterministically, so the adapted strata duplicate what residual does and buy nothing on top of residual-stratified.

## 8 Takeaways

- All four schemes are inverse-CDF sampling from the weight distribution; they differ only in how the $n$ uniforms are produced, and structure in the uniforms is free variance reduction.
- Residual and stratified provably beat multinomial on conditional variance, each by a single application of Jensen's inequality, and they compose: residual-stratified beats both.
- Systematic has no such guarantee. On an interleaved two-point population its conditional variance is $(\omega-\tfrac12)(1-\omega)\lvert f\rvert^2$ — no factor of $1/n$, so it does not vanish as the particle count grows.
- Stratified and systematic read the weights as an ordered sequence; residual and multinomial do not. Permuting the particles before a systematic resampling costs nothing and removes the known failure mode.
- Systematic is also the only scheme whose resampled particles are conditionally dependent, which is why it has no central limit theorem here and why analysing it is genuinely hard.
- The asymptotic variance decomposes additively into a resampling term and an importance-sampling term, which licenses treating the resampling scheme and the proposal as independent design problems.
- Using fewer particles after resampling than before makes the normalised variance look better without making the estimator better per unit of computation. The paper says so explicitly.
- There are no simulations in this paper. Everything here is what can be proved about one step, and the question of what it is worth over a long filter run is left open.
