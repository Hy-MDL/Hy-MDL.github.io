## Abstract

Sig-SDE makes the volatility process a linear combination of iterated integrals of time-augmented Brownian motion, truncated at a fixed depth. Because signatures form a linear basis for continuous functions of paths, the class can approximate Black–Scholes, local volatility, stochastic volatility or SABR while staying linear in its parameters. Three consequences follow: the price path is a linear functional of a *lead-lag* Brownian signature that does not depend on the parameters; payoffs written as linear functionals of the price signature are priced from one expected signature with no Monte Carlo; and calibration becomes minimisation of a polynomial in a few dozen coefficients. The proof of concept fits a depth-4, 31-parameter model to vanilla, variance and down-and-out barrier options generated by Black–Scholes with $\sigma=0.2$, then prices unseen down-and-in barrier puts. The algebra is the contribution; the evidence is one synthetic recovery.

**Keywords:** signature, expected signature, neural SDE, calibration, exotic derivatives, lead-lag transform, half-shuffle, rough paths

## 1 Introduction

Calibration usually starts by fixing a small parametric family — Black–Scholes, Heston, SABR — and fitting a handful of parameters to quoted prices. The fit is well posed because parameters are fewer than data points, but the hard decision is taken before any data is seen: which family. The machine-learning alternative inverts the trade-off: make volatility a neural network of the Brownian history, over-parameterise, and let gradient descent reprice the market — universality bought at the cost of Monte Carlo simulation of every instrument at every epoch.

Sig-SDE asks for a middle ground and states four requirements: **universality** (the class must contain or approximate the classical models), **efficient calibration**, **fast pricing** of exotics without Monte Carlo, and **cheap simulation**. The claim is that signatures deliver all four by linearising a non-linear problem on path space. The authors also reframe calibration as generative modelling: an SDE with a strong solution is a causal transport map from Brownian paths to price paths, and calibrating means picking that map — here from among maps that are linear on signatures — so that the law it pushes forward matches the market-implied pricing measure.

## 2 Background

Under the pricing measure with zero rates, a no-arbitrage price is a martingale,

$$
dX_t=\Sigma_t\,dW_t,\qquad X_0=x, \tag{1}
$$

with $W$ one-dimensional Brownian and $\Sigma_t$ adapted. Black–Scholes takes $\Sigma_t=\sigma X_t$, local volatility $\sigma(t,X_t)X_t$, stochastic volatility $\sigma_tX_t$, SABR $\sigma_tX_t^\beta$: all differ only in $\Sigma$.

The **signature** of a continuous semimartingale $X:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^d$ is the collection of iterated Stratonovich integrals indexed by words $K=\widehat K\otimes a$ over $\{1,\dots,d\}$:

$$
\mathbb{X}^{(K)}_{s,t}=\int_s^t \mathbb{X}^{(\widehat K)}_{s,u}\circ d X^{(a)}_u,\qquad \mathbb{X}^{(\varnothing)}_{s,t}=1. \tag{2}
$$

Level one is the increment, level two contains the Lévy area. Four properties carry the paper. <mark>Any continuous function on a compact set of paths is uniformly approximable by a linear functional of the signature</mark> — a Stone–Weierstrass argument, using that products of signature terms are again signature terms. The signature determines the path up to reparameterisation, and the *expected* signature determines the law, as moments do for a random variable. Terms decay like $1/n!$ in word length $n$, which makes truncation defensible. And the signature is invariant to time reparameterisation, which is why time must be added by hand as $\widehat W_t=(t,W_t)$ whenever calendar time matters.

Siblings: [Neural SDEs](/blog/neural-sde-pricing-hedging/) fits the same equation with neural coefficients and a Monte Carlo loss; [SigCWGAN](/blog/conditional-sig-wgan/) uses the expected signature as a distributional metric under $\mathbb{P}$ rather than as a pricing object under $\mathbb{Q}$.

## 3 Method

> **Key idea.** Write volatility as $\langle\ell,\text{signature of }(t,W_t)\rangle$. Every downstream quantity — price path, each term of the price signature, every option price, the calibration loss — then factors into an algebraic expression in $\ell$ times a Brownian quantity that is computed once and reused.

### 3.1 The Signature Model

With $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{0,t}$ the depth-$N$ signature of $\widehat W_t=(t,W_t)$,

$$
dX_t=\langle \ell,\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{0,t}\rangle\,dW_t,\qquad \ell=\{\ell^{(K)}:|K|\le N\}. \tag{3}
$$

$N$ plays the role of layer width and $\ell$ of weights; over the two letters of $\widehat W$, depth 4 gives $1+2+4+8+16=31$ coefficients. Two things are worth pinning down. $\Sigma_t$ is a functional of the *Brownian* path, not the price path, so non-Markovian volatility is automatic. And nothing keeps $\Sigma_t$ or $X_t$ positive: this is an arithmetic, not a geometric, model.

### 3.2 From Itô to lead-lag

Equation (3) is an Itô integral while signatures are Stratonovich, so (3) is not yet a linear statement about $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}$. The bridge is the **lead-lag transform**: the path is doubled into a copy running ahead and a copy running behind, and $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}$ is the limit of the signatures of those piecewise-linear doublings. Because the integrand sits on the lag copy while the integrator moves on the lead copy, integration against the lead-lag path reproduces the left-endpoint convention, so Itô integrals become linear functionals of $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}$. Quoting Lyons, Nejad and Perez Arribas,

$$
X_t=\big\langle x\,(\varnothing)+\ell\otimes(\text{lag}),\ \widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}_{0,t}\big\rangle, \tag{4}
$$

where $\ell\otimes(\text{lag})$ appends the lagged Brownian letter to every word of $\ell$. <mark>The price is linear in an object that does not depend on the parameters</mark>, so one simulated Brownian path can be repriced under any $\ell$ for the cost of an inner product. The step is exact; the approximations so far are the truncation at depth $N$ and the discretisation of the lead-lag limit.

### 3.3 The price signature and calibration

Earlier work shows exotic payoffs can be approximated by **signature payoffs** $\langle f,\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{0,T}\rangle$, linear functionals of the signature of $\widehat X=(t,X)$; their price is $\langle f,\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{0,T}]\rangle$. Pricing has collapsed into a pairing with one payoff-independent object, and the paper's contribution is a closed form for it. By induction on word length,

$$
\widehat{\mathbb{X}}^{(I)}_{s,t}=\big\langle C_I(\ell),\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}_{s,t}\big\rangle,\qquad C_I(\ell)=(\cdots(P_{i_1}\succ P_{i_2})\succ\cdots)\succ P_{i_n}, \tag{5}
$$

with $P_1=(1)$ the time letter, $P_2=\ell\otimes(\text{lag})$ the price letter from (4), and $\succ$ the **half-shuffle**, defined by $\langle F\succ G,\mathbb{X}_{s,t}\rangle=\int_s^t\langle F,\mathbb{X}_{s,u}\rangle\circ d\langle G,\mathbb{X}_{s,u}\rangle$ — the algebraic counterpart of integrating one functional against another. The induction is short: at length one, $\widehat{\mathbb{X}}^{(1)}=t-s$ and $\widehat{\mathbb{X}}^{(2)}=X_t-X_s$ are $P_1,P_2$ paired with $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}$; the step splits $I=J\otimes(i_n)$ and replaces the defining integral by a half-shuffle. Every step is exact.

The payoff is that each entry of $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{0,T}]$ equals $\langle C_I(\ell),\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}_{0,T}]\rangle$, and the expected lead-lag Brownian signature has a closed form (Appendix B.5): with $E_T=\exp\!\big(T(1)+\tfrac{T}{2}(2,2)\big)$ the expected signature of $\widehat W$, each entry is a finite sum over ways of cutting the word into blocks of length one or two, each block mapped by a rule that collapses the two Brownian letters onto one and sends mixed two-letter blocks to $\mp\tfrac12$ of the time letter — the Itô–Stratonovich correction, appearing as combinatorics. <mark>One table of Brownian numbers, computed once, prices every signature payoff under every $\ell$ with no Monte Carlo.</mark> Calibration to market prices $p_i$ of payoffs $\varphi_i$ is then

$$
\min_{\ell}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big\langle \varphi_i,\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{0,T}](\ell)\big\rangle-p_i\Big)^2. \tag{6}
$$

Since $C_I(\ell)$ is a product of $m$ copies of $\ell$, where $m$ counts price-letters in $I$, <mark>the model price is a polynomial in $\ell$ of degree at most $N$ and the objective one of degree at most $2N$ — degree 8 in 31 variables at $N=4$</mark>, linear only for payoffs supported on level-one words. With more coefficients than instruments the minimiser is not unique, which the authors read as the robust-finance case of several consistent martingale measures.

### 3.4 Intuition: Black–Scholes in this basis

Take the experiment's own target, $X_t=\exp(\sigma W_t-\tfrac12\sigma^2t)$, so $\Sigma_t=\sigma X_t$. Expanding in Hermite polynomials gives $X_t=\sum_k\sigma^kh_k(W_t,t)/k!$ with $h_0=1$, $h_1=W_t$, $h_2=W_t^2-t$, and the Stratonovich signature supplies exactly those monomials: $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{(1)}=t$, $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{(2)}=W_t$, $\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{(2,2)}=\tfrac12W_t^2$. So

$$
\Sigma_t=\sigma\,(\varnothing)+\sigma^2(2)+\sigma^3\big[(2,2)-\tfrac12(1)\big]+O(\sigma^4), \tag{7}
$$

a genuine element of the class: the coefficient on the word $(2)^{\otimes k}=W_t^k/k!$ is $\sigma^{k+1}$ — at $\sigma=0.2$, that is $0.2$, $0.04$, $0.008$ at levels 0–2 — and the first term a depth-4 truncation drops, $\sigma^6W_t^5/5!$, has standard deviation $\sigma^6\sqrt{945}/5!\approx1.6\times10^{-5}$ at $t=1$ (my arithmetic, not the paper's). Reported pricing errors of $10^{-4}$ are about six times larger, which suggests the residual is dominated by the signature-payoff approximation and the optimiser rather than by truncating $\ell$. The example also shows the honest sense of universality: Black–Scholes is not *in* the depth-4 model, it is approximated by it, with quality governed by $\sigma\sqrt T$.

### 3.5 Algorithm

```text
CALIBRATE(payoff coefficients phi_1..phi_n, prices p_1..p_n, depth N):
  for each maturity t in the instrument set:                  # parameter-free, once
      ES[t] <- expected lead-lag Brownian signature on [0,t]  # closed form
  define price_i(l):                                          # re-evaluated per step
      P1 <- (time letter);  P2 <- l tensor (lag letter)
      for each word I in support(phi_i):
          C_I(l) <- left-nested half-shuffle of P_{i_1},...,P_{i_n}
      return sum_I phi_i[I] * <C_I(l), ES[t_i]>
  return argmin_l sum_i ( price_i(l) - p_i )^2                # polynomial, degree <= 2N

SIMULATE(l, grid t_0<...<t_n, spot x, depth N):
  draw Brownian increments on the grid;  S <- unit signature;  X[0] <- x
  for k = 1..n:
      s_k <- signature of the lead-lag path on [t_{k-1}, t_k]
      S <- S * s_k                                            # Chen: glue segments
      X[k] <- < x*(empty word) + l tensor (lag letter), S >
  return X
```

The running product is the point of the paper's second simulation result: Chen's identity glues the signature of $[0,s]$ to that of $[s,t]$, so the whole path costs what the terminal value costs. Pricing needs no simulation at all — `SIMULATE` exists for scenario generation and hedging after calibration.

## 4 Implementation notes

| Item | As reported |
|---|---|
| Driving noise | one-dimensional; multi-dimensional extension asserted, not shown |
| Depth $N$ | 4 → 31 parameters |
| Spot, maturity | $X_0=1$, $T=1$ |
| Truncation in simulation | depth $N+1$ on the lead-lag alphabet |
| Libraries named | `esig`, `iisignature`, `signatory` |
| Optimiser, initialisation, time grid | **not stated** |
| Conversion of payoffs to $\varphi_i$ | **not stated** |
| Runtimes, hardware, baseline | **not stated** |

Two details are easy to get wrong. The first is the alphabet. Appendix B doubles a $d$-dimensional path into $2d$ coordinates, which for $(t,W)$ suggests four letters, and Algorithm 1 truncates accordingly; but the explicit expected-signature formula in B.5 is written over three letters, collapsing the second and third onto a common image and sending mixed two-letter blocks to $\pm\tfrac12$ of the time letter. Three — time, lead Brownian, lag Brownian — is the consistent reading, since doubling time is redundant under reparameterisation invariance, and it is the reading under which those corrections are the Itô–Stratonovich correction. A matching slip indexes the signature-payoff coefficients over three letters in §6 and two in §7; two is right, because $\widehat X=(t,X)$ has two coordinates. None of the algebra type-checks until these are fixed.

The second is cost. $C_I(\ell)$ for $|I|=n$ is an $n$-fold half-shuffle of functionals with up to 31 words each, and half-shuffling words of lengths $p$ and $q$ yields words of length $p+q$. Level-4 entries of $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{0,T}]$ therefore need entries of $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}^{LL}_{0,T}]$ at word lengths up to $4(N+1)=20$, with term counts growing combinatorially in depth and alphabet size. <mark>The paper gives no complexity bound and no timing anywhere</mark> — for a paper whose selling point is efficiency, the largest reproducibility gap.

## 5 Experiments

One experiment. "Market" prices come from Black–Scholes with $\sigma=0.2$, $X_0=1$, $T=1$; a depth-4 model is calibrated to three instrument families and used to price a fourth, held out. Errors are reported as heat maps, not numbers, so the table records the design and the top of each colour bar.

| Instrument | Payoff | Role | Grid | Top of colour bar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla call | $(X_t-K)^+$ | calibration | $K=0.5,\dots,1.1$; $t=0.4,\dots,1$ | $\approx2.5\times10^{-4}$ |
| Variance call | $(\langle X\rangle_t-K)^+$ | calibration | $K=0.01,\dots,0.04$; $t=0.4,\dots,1$ | $\approx6\times10^{-6}$ |
| Down-and-out call | $(X_t-K)^+$ if $\min_{s\le t}X_s>L$ | calibration | $K=0.9,\dots,1.03$; $L=0.6,\dots,0.9$; $t=1$ | $\approx6\times10^{-4}$ |
| Down-and-in put | $(K-X_t)^+$ if $\min_{s\le t}X_s<L$ | **held out** | $K=0.9,\dots,1.03$; $L=0.7,\dots,0.82$ | $\approx6\times10^{-4}$ |

There is no ablation, no baseline and no sensitivity study on $N$.

**Claim by claim.** *Universality* rests on the citation to Levin, Lyons and Ni plus the implicit evidence that depth 4 reproduces Black–Scholes prices to $10^{-4}$; §3.4 shows why that is the easiest possible target, so nothing tests rough or stochastic volatility. *Efficient calibration* is unsupported: no optimiser, iteration count or wall-clock. The introduction promises an "explicit solution … that completely removes the need to deploy expensive gradient descent" and calls the fit "a simple linear regression on the terms of the signature", while §7 delivers a non-convex polynomial minimisation of degree $2N$; <mark>the two descriptions are incompatible and are never reconciled</mark>. *Fast pricing without Monte Carlo* is supported in principle by (5) plus Appendix B.5 — the strongest part of the paper — but never timed. *Efficient simulation* is supported structurally by Chen's identity and illustrated by 1,000 paths ([Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00218#page=4)), again untimed. The one result with real content is generalisation: <mark>down-and-in barrier puts never used in calibration are priced with errors of the same order as the instruments that were</mark> ([Fig. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00218#page=4)), largest when barrier and strike are close, where the price depends most on the path law rather than the marginal. Two abstract claims have no body: *consistent calibration under $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{P}$* is two sentences in the conclusion, and the promised *convergence guarantees* appear nowhere.

## 6 Limitations

**Stated by the authors.**

- One-dimensional noise; the multi-dimensional extension is asserted, not carried out.
- With more parameters than instruments, (6) has many minimisers — framed as the robust-finance regime, but it does mean the calibrated model is unidentified.
- Exotic payoffs must first be approximated by signature payoffs, an approximation imported from earlier work.

**My reading.**

- The abstract oversells twice: convergence guarantees that never appear, and an "explicit solution" that is a non-convex polynomial minimisation.
- Parameter count is exponential in depth and in the number of drivers; two Brownian drivers at depth 4 would already need 121 coefficients, with half-shuffle expansions growing faster still.
- Nothing enforces $X_t>0$, and $\Sigma_t$ may change sign.
- The variance option is the awkward instrument. As far as I can tell the Stratonovich signature of $\widehat X=(t,X)$ is a continuous functional of the path $(t,X_t)$ — with one price coordinate there is no Lévy area to add information — while quadratic variation is not. So $\langle X\rangle_t$ is out of reach of Theorem 1.1 as stated, and the conversion presumably needs the price path lead-lag transformed too, as in the authors' earlier pricing paper. The paper never says how $\varphi_i$ is obtained for any family.
- Signature-payoff error and calibration error are never separated, so the reported $10^{-4}$ cannot be attributed.
- Two printed statements should be read against their sources. Chen's identity is typeset with both factors over the same interval $[0,t]$, which is the shuffle identity rather than Chen's; the correct version splits the interval, and Algorithm 1 uses the correct one. And $E_T$ is written $\exp(T+\tfrac T2(2,2))$, with the time letter dropped from the first summand. Neither affects the substance.

## 7 Extensions

**What was built on this.** The direct ancestor is Lyons, Nejad and Perez Arribas on non-parametric pricing and hedging with signature payoffs, which supplies (4) and the payoff-approximation step; Sig-SDE adds the model for $\Sigma$ and the closed form (5). The paper cites Gierjatowicz et al. on robust pricing and hedging with neural SDEs — the same equation with a network in place of $\langle\ell,\widehat{\mathbb{W}}\rangle$, calibrated by Monte Carlo, covered here as [Neural SDEs](/blog/neural-sde-pricing-hedging/) — and Cuchiero, Khosrawi and Teichmann on GAN calibration of local stochastic volatility. The expected signature returns as a distributional metric under $\mathbb{P}$ in [SigCWGAN](/blog/conditional-sig-wgan/); adversarial routes to the same generative problem are [Quant GANs](/blog/quant-gans/) and [SDE-GAN](/blog/neural-sde-gan/); the risk-side use of a calibrated simulator is [Deep Hedging](/blog/deep-hedging/).

**Open problems.** How depth-$N$ calibration error controls pricing error on payoffs outside the calibration set is unstated. There is no structure-preserving variant — positivity, or a parameterisation mapping to an implied volatility surface. The non-uniqueness of (6) is noted but not exploited: which minimiser, and how far apart are they on uncalibrated exotics? And the $\mathbb{Q}$-to-$\mathbb{P}$ step that any risk application needs is unspecified.

**Research directions.** *These are ideas, not results — none has been run.*

1. **Truncation spread as model risk.** Hypothesis: the spread of prices across the solution set of (6) is a usable proxy for model risk on an unhedged exotic. Data: listed SPX vanillas plus a variance swap strip. Baseline: the bid–ask-implied range under calibrated Heston. Metric: coverage of the realised exotic price. Failure mode: the solution set is unbounded in directions the payoff barely sees, making the interval vacuous.
2. **Rough volatility as the target.** Hypothesis: depth-4 Sig-SDE fails measurably on a rough Bergomi target with $H\approx0.1$, and the depth needed grows as $H$ falls. Data: prices simulated from rough Bergomi, per [Rough volatility](/blog/volatility-is-rough/) and [Deep calibration](/blog/deep-calibration-rough-vol/). Baseline: depth-$N$ fits for $N=2,\dots,6$. Metric: calibration and held-out barrier error against $N$ and $H$. Failure mode: prices fit while the path law is wrong, forcing the diagnostic onto path statistics.
3. **Joint $\mathbb{Q}$–$\mathbb{P}$ calibration.** Hypothesis: adding a signature-linear drift fitted to historical return moments, with $\ell$ frozen from the option fit, yields scenarios that are both arbitrage-consistent and heavy-tailed. Data: index options for $\mathbb{Q}$, daily returns for $\mathbb{P}$. Baseline: a market generator fitted on returns alone. Metric: tail quantile calibration plus option repricing error. Failure mode: the two fits conflict, which would itself be the informative result.

## 8 Takeaways

- Signatures give a linear basis on path space, so "volatility = linear functional of the Brownian signature" is a neural-SDE-shaped model whose parameters enter linearly at the level of the generator.
- The lead-lag transform is load-bearing: it turns Itô dynamics into the exact statement that the price, and every term of the price signature, is linear in a Brownian object independent of $\ell$.
- Pricing any signature payoff needs one closed-form expected signature, reused across instruments and parameters; calibration is then polynomial minimisation of degree $2N$ in 31 numbers — cheap in principle, but no optimiser, complexity or timing is reported.
- The worked Black–Scholes expansion shows both why the class is rich and why the experiment is weak: the target's volatility really does live in the basis with factorially decaying coefficients, which is exactly what makes depth 4 enough here.
- Evidence is one synthetic recovery plus one honest held-out barrier result. Treat efficiency and universality as plausible and well-motivated, not demonstrated.
- For financial time series this is the $\mathbb{Q}$-measure, option-calibrated counterpart of data-driven market generators: it needs quotes rather than long histories, at the price of saying nothing about the physical measure until a drift is added.
